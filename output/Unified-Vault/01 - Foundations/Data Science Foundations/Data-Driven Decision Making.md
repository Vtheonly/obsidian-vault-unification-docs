Data-driven decision making (DDDM) is the practice of supporting decisions with verifiable data evidence instead of intuition, experience, or authority alone. It is the paradigm shift that motivates data science as a discipline: models, statistics, and pipelines exist to reduce bias and uncertainty in the choices organizations make. This note covers the shift away from gut-feel decisions, the benefits that follow from it, and a healthcare case study that makes the contrast concrete.

## The shift from intuition to evidence

Historically, business and organizational decisions were made based on **intuition**, **experience**, or "gut feeling" — often distilled into the HIPPO, the *Highest Paid Person's Opinion*. Data science replaces this with **data-driven decision making (DDDM)**: decisions are supported by verifiable data evidence, which reduces bias and uncertainty. Direction is no longer set by the most senior voice in the room; claims are checked against what the data actually shows.

Anchoring decisions in data brings four key benefits:

1. **Increased efficiency** — optimizing operations (e.g., supply chain logistics) to reduce waste and cost.
2. **Personalized experiences** — using customer data to tailor recommendations (e.g., Netflix, Amazon), increasing satisfaction and retention.
3. **Risk mitigation** — predicting potential failures or fraud before they happen (e.g., credit card fraud detection).
4. **Strategic agility** — identifying market trends early to pivot business strategy ahead of competitors.

## Case study: healthcare

Consider a hospital managing patient records — the setting where the difference between the two approaches is easiest to see.

### Without data science: the traditional approach

In the traditional hospital, data is scattered across paper records or disconnected systems (silos). Doctors rely solely on their immediate observation and memory of the patient's history. Diagnosis is reactive: the patient is treated only after symptoms become severe.

### With data science: the modern approach

The modern approach changes what is possible at every step of care:

- **Integration:** all data (age, symptoms, history, genetics) is centralized.
- **Trend detection:** algorithms notice a spike in flu cases in a specific zip code.
- **Prediction:** a model predicts which patients are at high risk for sepsis or readmission within 30 days based on vitals.
- **Recommendation:** the system suggests personalized treatment plans based on what worked for similar historical patients.

> [!tip] The "why" matters
> Data science doesn't just provide numbers; it provides **context**. In the hospital example, the value isn't just knowing *how many* patients are sick, but knowing *which* patients need immediate preventative care to save lives.
