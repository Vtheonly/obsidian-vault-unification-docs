Succeeding in data science requires balancing two kinds of skills: "hard" (technical) skills and "soft" (interpersonal) skills. The technical side — programming, mathematics, data manipulation and visualization, and machine learning — is what lets you build an analysis; the soft side is what ensures the analysis answers the right question and reaches the people who act on it. Neither half is optional.

## Technical skills

### Programming

Python is the dominant language for data science, prized for its simplicity and its massive library ecosystem (Pandas, NumPy). R remains popular in academia and pure statistics. SQL (Structured Query Language) is **non-negotiable**: you must know how to talk to databases to get your data in the first place. The Python side of this toolkit is mapped in [[Python Language Overview]], [[NumPy]], and [[Pandas]].

### Mathematics

Three mathematical areas underpin the work. **Statistics** covers distributions, p-values, and hypothesis testing. **Linear algebra** — understanding vectors and matrices — is crucial for deep learning. **Calculus** supplies rates of change (derivatives), the basis of optimization. These foundations are developed in [[Probability and Statistics for ML]], [[Linear Algebra Fundamentals]], and [[Derivatives and the Chain Rule]].

### Data manipulation and visualization

Real data arrives messy, so wrangling — cleaning messy data, typically with Pandas — is a core competency. On the visualization side, Python offers Matplotlib and Seaborn, while Tableau and Power BI provide drag-and-drop alternatives; [[Matplotlib]] and [[Seaborn]] cover the code-first tools.

### Machine learning

Finally, a working knowledge of machine learning is expected: understanding the main algorithm families (regression, classification, clustering) and the evaluation metrics (accuracy, precision, recall) used to judge model quality. [[Introduction to Machine Learning]] and [[Types of Learning]] introduce those families.

## Soft skills

> [!warning] Crucial for career growth
> Many junior data scientists fail not because they can't code, but because they can't communicate.

**Communication** is the ability to explain complex technical concepts to non-technical stakeholders — for instance, explaining a neural network to a marketing manager.

**Critical thinking** means asking the right questions before building a model: "Is the data biased?", "Does this solve the actual business problem?"

**Curiosity** is the desire to dig deeper. When you see an anomaly in the data, do you ignore it, or do you investigate *why* it's there?

**Ethics** means understanding the impact of your models — for example, asking whether your hiring algorithm is biased against a certain gender or race.
