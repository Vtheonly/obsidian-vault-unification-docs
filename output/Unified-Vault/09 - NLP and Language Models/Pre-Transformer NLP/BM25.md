BM25 (Okapi BM25, Robertson et al. 1995) is the standard scoring function for keyword search: given a query and a document, it produces a relevance score from simple term statistics. It improves on [[TF-IDF]] with two ideas — term frequency saturation and document length normalization — and despite being roughly 30 years old it remains the go-to system for keyword retrieval and a critical component of hybrid RAG. This note covers the formula and its parameters, the two improvements over TF-IDF, tuning, variants, worked implementations, and BM25's role alongside vector search.

> [!info] TL;DR
> BM25 (Okapi BM25, Robertson et al. 1995) is the standard scoring function for keyword search. It improves on TF-IDF with term saturation and document length normalization. It is still the standard for keyword retrieval in 2026 — and a critical component of hybrid RAG.

## The scoring formula

For a query $q$ with terms $q_1, \ldots, q_n$ and a document $d$:

$$
\text{BM25}(q, d) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{IDF}(q_i) \cdot \frac{\text{tf}(q_i, d) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{\text{tf}(q_i, d) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}
$$

Where:

- $\text{tf}(q_i, d)$: term frequency of $q_i$ in $d$.
- $|d|$: length of document $d$.
- $\text{avgdl}$: average document length in the corpus.
- $k_1$: term frequency saturation parameter (typically 1.2–2.0).
- $b$: document length normalization (typically 0.75).

The score is a sum over query terms: each term contributes its IDF weight times a saturated, length-normalized term frequency.

### The BM25 IDF

BM25's IDF differs from the one used in classical TF-IDF. TF-IDF's IDF is $\log(N / df)$ or $\log(N / (1 + df))$, where $N$ is the number of documents in the corpus and $df$ the number of documents containing the term. BM25 instead uses a probabilistic formulation with smoothing:

$$
\text{idf}(t) = \log\left(\frac{N - df + 0.5}{df + 0.5} + 1\right)
$$

This is the form implemented in the worked example below. BM25's IDF can be negative (when a term appears in more than half the documents), which TF-IDF's can't be (with the +1 smoothing). The smoothing prevents extreme values for very common or very rare terms. Conceptually, IDF is related to information content — a term concentrated in a few documents carries more evidence than one scattered everywhere.

## The two improvements over TF-IDF

BM25 improves on TF-IDF in two specific ways, and together they fix the two systematic biases of linear term weighting.

### Term frequency saturation

In raw TF-IDF, $\text{tf}$ grows linearly: a word appearing 100 times scores 10x a word appearing 10 times, and a document with 10 occurrences of "cat" scores 10× higher than one with a single occurrence. But after the first few occurrences, more occurrences don't really make the document more relevant.

BM25 replaces the linear term frequency with a saturation function:

$$
\text{TF saturation} = \frac{(k_1 + 1) \cdot f(t, d)}{k_1 + f(t, d)}
$$

This saturates at $k_1 + 1$ as $f(t, d) \to \infty$; equivalently, BM25's $\frac{\text{tf} \cdot (k_1+1)}{\text{tf} + k_1}$ asymptotes to $k_1 + 1$ as tf → ∞. The first occurrence matters most; subsequent occurrences add diminishing returns. With $k_1 = 1.2$, the function reaches ~50% of its max at $f = 1$, ~75% at $f = 3$, and ~90% at $f = 10$: more occurrences still help, but far less than linearly.

### Document length normalization

Longer documents naturally have higher term frequencies — there are more chances to mention a term — and without normalization, long docs always rank higher. In TF-IDF, longer documents are unfairly favored for exactly this reason.

BM25's $(1 - b + b \cdot |d|/\text{avgdl})$ factor normalizes for length — as a display equation:

$$
\text{Length norm} = 1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}
$$

Here $b = 1$ means full normalization and $b = 0$ means no normalization; $b = 0.75$ is the standard. With $b = 0.75$, a document 2× the average length gets a 1.75× penalty on term frequency, which ensures fair comparison across documents of different lengths.

### Why these matter

Without saturation, a 1000-word document with 50 mentions of "cat" would dominate search results, even if a 100-word document with 3 mentions is more relevant. Without length normalization, long documents always win. BM25 fixes both with two intuitive parameters: $k_1$ for saturation, $b$ for length normalization.

## Why BM25 still wins

Despite being 30 years old, BM25 remains the standard for keyword search. It nails exact-match queries like `error code 0xC0000005`, where the exact tokens are the whole point. It requires no training — it works out of the box on any corpus. Its scores are interpretable, because they decompose by term. It is fast: inverted indexes (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Lucene) make BM25 sub-millisecond per query. And it is cheap — no model to run, no embeddings to compute.

## BM25 vs vector search

The trade-off between lexical and semantic retrieval is easiest to see side by side:

| Property                | BM25                          | Vector Search                     |
|-------------------------|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Match type              | Exact token                   | Semantic                          |
| Misspellings            | Misses                        | Can match (depending on embedder) |
| Synonyms                | Misses                        | Matches                           |
| Long-tail terms         | Strong                        | Often weak (embedders undertrain) |
| Speed                   | Very fast (inverted index)    | Slower (vector similarity)        |
| Cost                    | Cheap                         | More expensive (embedding compute)|
| Setup                   | Just index                    | Need embedding model              |

The practical answer is **both**. Hybrid search (BM25 + vector, with reranking) is the production standard: BM25 catches exact-match queries, and vector search catches semantic ones.

## BM25 in modern hybrid RAG

BM25 is not obsolete — as of 2026 it is a critical component of hybrid RAG systems. Five properties keep it relevant. Exact match: BM25 finds exact keyword matches (product codes, names, error messages) that embeddings miss. Rare terms: BM25 handles rare terms well, while embeddings may not have seen them in training. Interpretability: BM25 scores are explainable — you can see which terms contributed. Speed: BM25 is faster than embedding-based search (inverted index vs. ANN). And no model is needed: BM25 doesn't require a trained model, just statistics.

### The hybrid search pattern

A minimal hybrid retriever pulls an oversized candidate set from both systems and fuses the rankings:

```python
def hybrid_search(query, top_k=10, bm25_weight=0.3, embedding_weight=0.7):
    # BM25 search
    bm25_scores = bm25_index.search(query, top_k=top_k * 2)
    # Embedding search
    query_emb = embedding_model.encode(query)
    embedding_scores = vector_index.search(query_emb, top_k=top_k * 2)
    # Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)
    fused = reciprocal_rank_fusion(bm25_scores, embedding_scores)
    return fused[:top_k]

def reciprocal_rank_fusion(bm25_results, embedding_results, k=60):
    """RRF: rank-based fusion that doesn't need score calibration."""
    scores = {}
    for rank, (doc_id, _) in enumerate(bm25_results):
        scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0) + 1 / (k + rank)
    for rank, (doc_id, _) in enumerate(embedding_results):
        scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0) + 1 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])
```

Two methods combine the result lists. Score fusion takes a weighted sum of normalized BM25 and embedding scores; it requires calibration, because the scores from the two retrievers come from different distributions. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) instead computes $\text{score}(d) = \sum_r 1/(k + \text{rank}_r(d))$, where $r$ indexes the retrievers and $k = 60$; it is rank-based, doesn't need score calibration, and is the standard for hybrid RAG — most production systems use it.

### When BM25 wins over embeddings

BM25 wins on exact match queries like "iPhone 15 Pro Max 256GB": it finds the exact product code, while embeddings may match semantically similar but wrong products. It wins on rare entity names like "Zbigniew Brzezinski", which embeddings may never have seen in training. It wins on error messages like "Error 404: NotFound", where embeddings tend to surface similar-but-wrong errors, and on code search, where `function_name()` calls for the exact function rather than a lookalike.

### When embeddings win over BM25

Embeddings win on semantic queries: "how to fix a slow computer" should match a document about "performance optimization", and BM25 may miss if the document doesn't use those exact words. They win on paraphrasing: "laptop" vs. "notebook computer" are matched semantically, while BM25 requires exact terms. And they win across languages: "chat" (French) vs. "cat" (English) — embeddings cross languages; BM25 doesn't.

## Worked examples

### Scoring with rank-bm25

The `rank_bm25` package gives BM25 in a few lines:

```python
from rank_bm25 import BM25Okapi

docs = [
    "the cat sat on the mat".split(),
    "the dog ran in the park".split(),
    "cats and dogs are common pets".split(),
]
bm25 = BM25Okapi(docs)

query = "cat on mat".split()
scores = bm25.get_scores(query)
print(scores)  # highest for docs[0]
```

For production, use Elasticsearch / OpenSearch / Meilisearch / Tantivy — they implement BM25 efficiently with inverted indexes.

### A complete implementation

The whole algorithm fits in one small class, and reading it makes the formula concrete:

```python
import math
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

class BM25:
    def __init__(self, documents, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
        self.k1 = k1
        self.b = b
        self.documents = documents
        self.doc_len = [len(doc.split()) for doc in documents]
        self.avgdl = sum(self.doc_len) / len(self.doc_len)
        self.doc_freqs = [Counter(doc.split()) for doc in documents]
        self.N = len(documents)
        # Inverse document frequency
        self.idf = self._compute_idf()

    def _compute_idf(self):
        """IDF with BM25 smoothing: idf(t) = log((N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) + 1)"""
        idf = {}
        for term in set().union(*[set(df.keys()) for df in self.doc_freqs]):
            df = sum(1 for doc_freq in self.doc_freqs if term in doc_freq)
            idf[term] = math.log((self.N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) + 1)
        return idf

    def score(self, query, doc_idx):
        """BM25 score for a query against a specific document."""
        score = 0.0
        doc_freq = self.doc_freqs[doc_idx]
        doc_len = self.doc_len[doc_idx]
        for term in query.split():
            if term not in self.idf:
                continue
            f = doc_freq.get(term, 0)
            if f == 0:
                continue
            # Term frequency saturation
            tf = (f * (self.k1 + 1)) / (f + self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * doc_len / self.avgdl))
            score += self.idf[term] * tf
        return score

    def search(self, query, top_k=5):
        """Return top-k documents for the query."""
        scores = [(i, self.score(query, i)) for i in range(self.N)]
        scores.sort(key=lambda x: -x[1])
        return [(self.documents[i], score) for i, score in scores[:top_k]]

# Usage
docs = [
    "the cat sat on the mat",
    "the dog sat on the log",
    "cats and dogs are pets",
    "the cat chased the mouse",
]
bm25 = BM25(docs)
results = bm25.search("cat sat", top_k=2)
# Returns docs with "cat" and "sat" — high BM25 score
```

The `_compute_idf` method implements the smoothed probabilistic IDF, and `score` applies saturation and length normalization exactly as in the formula above.

## Tuning k1 and b

The two BM25 parameters control its behavior, and both have well-chosen defaults.

$k_1$ (term frequency saturation) ranges typically from 1.0 to 2.0, with a default of 1.2. Lower values (1.0, or 0.5–1.0 for shorter documents) make term frequency saturate faster — even 2–3 occurrences give a near-max score. Higher values (2.0, or 1.5–2.0 for longer documents) make term frequency matter more — more occurrences keep helping. For short queries (1–3 words), $k_1 = 1.2$ is good; for long queries (5+ words), k1 = 1.5-2.0 may help.

$b$ (document length normalization) ranges from 0 to 1, with a default of 0.75. $b = 0$ means no length normalization — long and short documents are treated equally. $b = 1$ means full length normalization — term frequency is fully divided by length. $b = 0.5$ is moderate. In practice, lower $b$ (0.5) gives long documents a chance, while higher $b$ (0.9) penalizes them more. For collections with uniform document length, $b = 0.5$ is fine; for collections with highly variable lengths (web pages, articles), the default $b = 0.75$ is good.

Most production systems use the defaults ($k_1 = 1.2$, $b = 0.75$). Tuning gives a 1–5% improvement on retrieval benchmarks — often not worth the effort. Most users don't tune these at all: the defaults are well-chosen, and you should tune only if you have an evaluation pipeline. If you do tune, use a validation set with relevance judgments and grid search over $k_1 \in \{1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0\}$ and $b \in \{0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$.

## Variants

Three named variants refine the basic formula. **BM25F** is per-field BM25, with different weighting for title, body, and other fields. **BM25+** addresses the "over-penalization" of long documents. **BM25L** is another length-normalization variant. For most use cases, plain BM25 (Okapi) is fine.

## Why this matters for AI

Every RAG system should consider BM25: pure vector search misses exact-match queries — product codes, error messages, names, IDs — and BM25 catches them. BM25 is the baseline that vector search must beat for keyword-heavy queries, and often it doesn't. For hybrid RAG, BM25 + vector + cross-encoder reranker is the production standard. BM25 is also infrastructure-level: Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Postgres (via `tsvector`), Tantivy, and Meilisearch all implement it, so knowing it lets you read IR papers and tune real search systems.

## Production implications

Use an inverted-index engine for BM25 at scale: Elasticsearch or OpenSearch for managed deployments, Tantivy or Meilisearch for self-hosted. Index fields separately (title, body, metadata) so you can weight them differently. For hybrid scoring, combine BM25 and vector scores with a weighted sum or learned fusion (e.g., Reciprocal Rank Fusion). Tune $k_1$ and $b$ only with a labeled eval set — don't tune blind. And for multi-language corpora, remember that BM25 works per-language if you tokenize correctly; CJK needs special handling.

## Common pitfalls and failure modes

The classic pitfalls are preprocessing mistakes. With no stemming or lemmatization, "running" and "run" are different tokens — use a stemmer (Porter, Snowball) or a lemmatizer. With no stop-word removal, common words dominate the score. Treating BM25 as ground truth is another error — it's a baseline, and for semantic queries vector search wins. Forgetting to re-index when the corpus changes silently serves stale results, because BM25 indexes must be updated when documents change. And using the wrong analyzer for the language breaks retrieval — an English analyzer doesn't handle Chinese, Arabic, and so on correctly.

When something goes wrong, the failure modes are recognizable:

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| BM25 returns irrelevant results | No stemming/lemmatization | Apply stemming (Porter); lowercase; stopword removal |
| Rare terms not found | IDF smoothing too aggressive | Use standard BM25 IDF formula; check vocabulary |
| Long documents always win | Length normalization too weak | Increase b (toward 1.0); check avgdl computation |
| BM25 misses semantic matches | BM25 is lexical, not semantic | Use hybrid search (BM25 + embeddings) |
| BM25 too slow | Naive implementation | Use inverted index (Elasticsearch, Lucene); precompute IDF |
| BM25 scores not comparable across queries | IDF depends on corpus | Use the same corpus for all queries; don't compare raw scores |

## Interview questions

**What are the two improvements BM25 makes over TF-IDF?**
Term frequency saturation: BM25 uses $\frac{(k_1+1) \cdot f}{k_1 + f}$ instead of linear TF — more occurrences help with diminishing returns, not linearly. Document length normalization: BM25 divides TF by $1 - b + b \cdot |d|/\text{avgdl}$ — longer documents get a penalty so they don't dominate. Without these, TF-IDF favors long documents with many term occurrences.

**What do the BM25 parameters k1 and b control?**
k1 (default 1.2) controls term frequency saturation — higher k1 means more occurrences keep helping; lower k1 means faster saturation. b (default 0.75) controls document length normalization — b = 0 means no normalization; b = 1 means full normalization. Most systems use the defaults; tuning gives a 1–5% improvement, often not worth the effort.

**Why is BM25 still used in 2026 despite embedding-based search?**
Four reasons. (1) Exact match: BM25 finds exact keywords (product codes, names, errors) that embeddings miss. (2) Rare terms: BM25 handles rare terms; embeddings may not have seen them. (3) Interpretability: BM25 scores are explainable. (4) Speed: BM25 (inverted index) is faster than ANN search. Modern RAG uses hybrid search (BM25 + embeddings with reciprocal rank fusion) to get the best of both.

**How does hybrid search combine BM25 and embedding results?**
Two methods. (1) Score fusion: a weighted sum of normalized BM25 and embedding scores; it requires calibration, because the scores come from different distributions. (2) Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF): $\text{score}(d) = \sum_r 1/(k + \text{rank}_r(d))$ where $r$ indexes retrievers and $k=60$. RRF is rank-based, doesn't need score calibration, and is the standard for hybrid RAG — most production systems use it.

**When does BM25 beat embedding-based search?**
Four cases. (1) Exact match queries (product codes, names, error messages) — BM25 finds exact matches; embeddings may match semantically similar but wrong items. (2) Rare entity names — BM25 finds exact names; embeddings may not have seen them. (3) Error messages — BM25 finds exact errors; embeddings match similar errors. (4) Code search (function names) — BM25 finds exact functions; embeddings match similar functions.

**How does BM25's IDF differ from TF-IDF's IDF?**
TF-IDF IDF is $\log(N / df)$ or $\log(N / (1 + df))$. BM25 IDF is $\log((N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) + 1)$ — a probabilistic formulation with smoothing. BM25's IDF can be negative (when a term appears in more than half the documents), which TF-IDF's can't be (with the +1 smoothing). The smoothing prevents extreme values for very common or very rare terms.
